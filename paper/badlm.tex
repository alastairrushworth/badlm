\title{Bayesian adaptive distributed lag models}
\author{Alastair Rushworth}
\date{First version: January 2018 (arXiv:1801.06670v1). Revised \today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Distributed lag models (DLMs) express the cumulative and delayed dependence
between pairs of time-indexed response and explanatory variables. In practical
application, users of DLMs examine the estimated influence of a series of
lagged covariates to assess patterns of dependence. Much recent methodological
work has sought to develop flexible parameterisations for smoothing the
associated lag parameters that avoid overfitting. However, this paper finds
that some widely-used DLMs introduce bias in the estimated lag influence, and
are sensitive to the maximum lag which is typically chosen in advance of model
fitting. Simulations show that bias and misspecification are dramatically
reduced by generalising the smoothing model to allow varying penalisation of
the lag influence estimates. The resulting model is shown to have substantially
fewer effective parameters and lower bias, providing the user with confidence
that the estimates are robust to prior model choice. An efficient inference
scheme is described in which the lag coefficients are integrated out
analytically and the remaining low-dimensional posterior is sampled with the
No-U-Turn Sampler, with a cost per likelihood evaluation that does not grow
with the length of the series.

\medskip\noindent
\textit{Keywords:} Distributed lag model; penalized spline; adaptive smoothing
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Distributed lag models (DLMs) find application whenever the influence of a
time-indexed independent variable is delayed and spread over time. Although
DLMs are widely applicable, their development was originally motivated by
problems in econometrics \citep{nerlove1958, almon1965, zellner1970, haugh1977}
and they have more recently experienced a surge in popularity in environmental
epidemiology \citep{zanobetti2000, welty2009, gasparrini2010}. Crucially, DLMs
enable direct interpretation of the influence of a temporal exposure ensemble,
which is particularly useful for characterising the total health impact of
persistent environmental exposures such as air pollution or temperature
\citep{wyzga1978, gasparrini2010}. Lag models can help to identify subtle types
of time-dependence such as `mortality displacement', which occurs when exposure
related mortality diminishes a vulnerable subpopulation, resulting in lower
mortality in subsequent time intervals. Mortality displacement has been widely
documented \citep{schwartz2000, braga2001} and is conspicuous where lag
influence is estimated to have a protective effect at low or moderate lags for
what are otherwise harmful exposures \citep{zanobetti2000}. Correctly
identifying these effects depends on obtaining unbiased estimates of the
underlying lag influence, and it is therefore essential that the DLM is
correctly specified. Since no previous study has formally quantified the
estimation cost of model misspecification, this paper provides a comparison of
various existing techniques.

If a time-varying explanatory variable $X_t$ has some influence on a response
variable $Y_t$ over a set of lags $0, \ldots, p$, then a simple DLM can be
expressed as the regression model $Y_t = \sum_{i=0}^{p} X_{t-i} \beta_i +
\epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is an independent error process and the parameters
$\bbeta = (\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_p)$ are referred to as the `lag
curve' or lag function, as a function of $i$. $\bbeta$ cannot be reliably
estimated using ordinary generalised linear models when $X_t$ and $X_{t-1}$ are
correlated, and regularisation using shape constraints or smoothness
restrictions over the lag parameters $\bbeta$ are frequently used. Recent
studies have implemented various forms of flexible parameterisation using
polynomial splines and smoothness penalties, for example by combining uniformly
spaced B-splines with ridge penalisation \citep{zanobetti2000},
logarithmically spaced B-splines \citep{gasparrini2010}, penalised B-splines
\citep{rushworth2013, obermeier2015}, and Bayesian penalised splines with
varying ridge penalties \citep{muggeo2008}. Spline basis representations ensure
a minimum level of smoothness, and offer computational relief when there are
fewer knots than lags, which reduces the total number of parameters to
estimate. Although smoothness restrictions primarily serve to stabilise the
model, they can also be exploited to incorporate additional structure over the
shape of the estimated lag curve. For example, it is widely assumed that the
true lag curve has more curvature at short lags than at large lags, reflecting
the intuition that recent events are generally the most influential, and
influence should decay rapidly to zero for increasing lags. Further structure
can also be used to avoid discontinuity between the influence of the final
modelled lag $\beta_p \neq 0$ and the implicitly assumed $\beta_{p+j} = 0$.
Some examples include \citet{koyck1954} who restricted $\bbeta$ to decay
geometrically with increasing $p$; \citet{muggeo2008} who combined a B-spline
basis with parameters which were shrunk by a lag-varying ridge penalty;
\citet{gasparrini2010} used logarithmic knot placement in the lag dimension to
enable varying smoothness; and \citet{welty2009} constructed a Gaussian process
prior distribution that enforces smaller covariances on lag parameters at short
lags than longer ones. While each of these approaches achieves a level of
lag-varying smoothness, each incorporates a parametric assumption on the type
of penalisation that occurs, which may not be appropriate in general.

A further problem arises because the maximum lag $p$ must be chosen before
model fitting: this is sometimes justified by drawing on previously published
studies, for example, in several studies of the delayed effects of temperature
on human health, $20 \leq p \leq 30$ is consistently reported
\citep{braga2002, armstrong2006, gasparrini2013}. In general, there is a cost
to getting $p$ wrong: too large and the model risks overfitting, and too small
can bias the resulting lag function estimate. Few studies have investigated
sensitivity of different models to choices of $p$, although several authors
recognise that this is an ongoing difficulty for DLMs. \citet{heaton2012} and
\citet{heaton2014} provide notable exceptions, and estimate $p$ as part of a
two-stage approach. However, DLMs are often applied in settings where the true
lag curve is believed to decay gradually to 0, and in such settings, $p$ is
likely to be poorly estimated and will lack a meaningful interpretation.

This paper proposes a pragmatic strategy that ensures flexible and automatic
smoothing over the lag structure that avoids the need to either estimate or
choose $p$ in advance. By exploiting the property that very large lags have
close to 0 influence, an automatic lag-dependent smoother is constructed that
ensures strong penalisation at high lags so that $p$ can be made arbitrarily
large without the risk of overfitting. This follows the tradition of richly
specifying a hierarchical model and ensuring parsimony by liberal use of
regularisation and smoothing. A new DLM is described in Section
\ref{sec:model}, its computation in Section \ref{sec:comp}, and in Section
\ref{sec:sim} a simulation study
illustrates the DLM's desirable properties, especially in comparison to
existing approaches. This study compares bias resulting from getting $p$
wrong, which is itself a central contribution, as DLMs have not previously
been compared in this manner. Section \ref{sec:real} applies the model
to four real data sets: daily stock market returns, intraday order flow and
prices at one-second resolution, air pollution and mortality in Chicago, and
COVID-19 cases and deaths.
Section \ref{sec:discussion} concludes the paper with an overview of the main
findings and a discussion of the implications for users of distributed lag
models.

\section{Adaptive distributed lag models}
\label{sec:model}

Define $\by = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)$, a response variable time series of length
$n$, and $\bx = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$, a time series of an exposure or input,
whose influence on $\by$ up to some lag $p$ is to be estimated. A Gaussian DLM
for these data can be written as $y_i \sim \N\left(\sum_{j=0}^{p} x_{i-j}
\beta_j,\ \sigma^2\right)$ where $i = p+1, p+2, \ldots, n$, and $\sigma^2$ is
the error variance. The lag parameters $(\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_p)$
could be penalised and estimated directly, but for ease of comparison with
existing approaches, these will be projected onto a B-spline basis of $K$
functions uniformly spaced over the lag range $0, \ldots, p$. The basis
representation can be expressed as $\beta_j = \sum_{k=1}^{K} B_k(j) b_k$ where
the further set of parameters $\bb = (b_1, \ldots, b_K)$ determine the fitted
lag curve. The model can be expressed as $\by \sim \N(\bX \bb,\ \sigma^2
\bI)$, where $\bX_{ik} = \sum_{j=0}^{p} x_{i-j} B_k(j)$. To control the
roughness of $b_k$ in a way that enables variable smoothing, a similar
approach to \citet{brewer2007} and \citet{reich2008} is used, in which the
intrinsic autoregressive (ICAR) prior distribution of \citet{besag1991} is
generalised. The conditional prior distribution for $\bb$ under the ordinary
ICAR is $\bb \sim \N(\bzero, \frac{1}{\lambda} \bP^{-})$, resulting in a
conditional prior density of
\begin{equation}
\pi(\bb \mid \lambda) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2} \bb^\top \bP \bb\right)
= \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^{K-1} \lambda (b_{k+1} - b_k)^2\right).
\label{eq:icar}
\end{equation}
The precision parameter $\lambda$ controls the extent to which the squared
differences of consecutive pairs of $\bb$ influence the posterior, imparting a
smoothing effect over the lag curve derived from $\bb$. Since each squared
difference $(b_{k+1} - b_k)^2$ contributes equally in the prior in Equation
\eqref{eq:icar}, variable smoothing can be achieved naturally by introducing a
set of precision parameters $\blambda = (\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_{K-1})$
that enables pairs of B-spline parameters to be smoothed more or less
strongly. This generalised prior can be written $\pi(\bb \mid \blambda)
\propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^{K-1} \lambda_k (b_{k+1} -
b_k)^2\right) = \N(\bzero, \bQ^{-})$ where $\bQ = \bD^\top \mathrm{diag}
(\blambda) \bD$, with $\bD$ the $(K-1) \times K$ first-difference matrix, is
the precision matrix
\begin{equation*}
\bQ =
\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda_1 & -\lambda_1 & 0 & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\
-\lambda_1 & \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 & -\lambda_2 & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\
0 & -\lambda_2 & \lambda_2 + \lambda_3 & -\lambda_3 & \ldots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -\lambda_{K-1} & \lambda_{K-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
$\bQ$ has rank $K - 1$ and its null space is spanned by the constant vector,
so the prior is intrinsic: it is improper with respect to the overall level of
$\bb$ but proper for the differences, and $\bQ^{-}$ denotes a generalised
inverse. The posterior distribution is nevertheless proper, since the level of
$\bb$ is identified by the likelihood. This joint specification for $\bb$
results in the conditional distributions
\begin{equation*}
b_k \mid \bb_{-k}, \blambda \sim
\begin{cases}
\N\left(b_{k+1},\ \frac{1}{\lambda_1}\right) & \text{if } k = 1 \\[6pt]
\N\left(\frac{\lambda_{k-1} b_{k-1} + \lambda_k b_{k+1}}{\lambda_{k-1} + \lambda_k},\
\frac{1}{\lambda_{k-1} + \lambda_k}\right) & \text{if } k = 2, \ldots, K-1 \\[6pt]
\N\left(b_{k-1},\ \frac{1}{\lambda_{K-1}}\right) & \text{if } k = K.
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
When $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \ldots = \lambda_{K-1} = \lambda$, then $\bQ =
\lambda \bP$, which shows that the ordinary ICAR prior in Equation
\eqref{eq:icar} is a special case of the adaptive prior.

The influence of very large lags is expected to be negligible, and a lag
curve that terminates away from zero at lag $p$ would imply an artificial
discontinuity with the implicitly assumed $\beta_{p+j} = 0$. The constraint
$\beta_p = 0$, optionally together with $\beta'_p = 0$, is therefore imposed
exactly. Writing the constraints as $\bC \bb = \bzero$, where the $m \times
K$ matrix $\bC$ holds the basis values and derivatives at lag $p$, and
letting the orthonormal columns of the $K \times (K - m)$ matrix $\bZ$ span
the null space of $\bC$, the coefficients are parameterised as $\bb = \bZ
\tilde{\bb}$, so that the basis becomes $\bX \bZ$ and the prior precision of
$\tilde{\bb}$ becomes $\tilde{\bQ} = \bZ^\top \bQ \bZ$. The null space of
$\bQ$ is spanned by the constant vector, which does not satisfy the value
constraint, so $\tilde{\bQ}$ is positive definite and the prior on
$\tilde{\bb}$ is proper. The constraint adds no tuning parameter and, as
shown in Section \ref{sec:comp}, leaves the normalising constant of the prior
in closed form.

A further smoothing prior distribution is placed over $\blambda$ to restrict
their flexibility. This is particularly attractive as it also reflects our
intuition that the curvature of the lag function should not change too
rapidly. For this purpose, an ordinary ICAR prior is used to smooth the log
precisions $\btau = (\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_{K-1})$, $\tau_k = \log
\lambda_k$, such that $\btau \mid \zeta^2 \sim \N(\bzero, \zeta^2 \bK^{-})$
where $\bK$ is the $(K-1) \times (K-1)$ precision matrix
\begin{equation*}
\bK =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & -1 & 0 & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\
-1 & 2 & -1 & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\
0 & -1 & 2 & -1 & \ldots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \ldots & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, for small values of the variance $\zeta^2$, pairs of consecutive
elements of $\btau$ are more strongly smoothed towards each other. This random
walk is itself intrinsic, with rank $K - 2$, and is anchored by a prior on the
level $\bar{\tau}$ of the log precisions. Both this and the prior on $\zeta$
are penalised complexity priors \citep{simpson2017}. The scale $\zeta$ has an
exponential prior with rate chosen so that $\Pr(\zeta > 2) = 0.1$, which
shrinks the model towards its non-adaptive special case unless the data ask
for adaptivity. The standard deviation $\exp(-\bar{\tau}/2)$ of coefficient
differences implied by the level has an exponential prior with
$\Pr\{\exp(-\bar{\tau}/2) > 1\} = 0.05$ on standardised data; its density
decays like $\exp(-\bar{\tau}/2)$ for large precisions, which prevents the
precision from drifting without bound once the data no longer inform it, as
happens wherever the true curve is exactly flat. The error variance has
$\sigma^2 \sim \mbox{Inverse-Gamma}(0.001, 0.001)$, and unpenalised terms,
an intercept and any covariates, have flat priors. Both $\bx$ and $\by$ are
standardised before fitting and every reported quantity is transformed back
to the original scale. The combination of adaptive smoothing with smoothed
precisions results in a very flexible model that can accommodate complex
shapes using a relatively simple specification. The model described above
assumes Gaussian errors for simplicity; autocorrelated errors are treated in
Section \ref{sec:comp}, and the ideas generalise to other response
distributions.

\section{Computation}
\label{sec:comp}

\subsection{Integrating out the lag coefficients}
\label{sec:collapse}

A Gibbs sampler that alternates between the coefficients $\bb$ and the log
precisions $\btau$, with Metropolis steps for the latter, is the obvious
scheme for this model and was used in the first version of this paper. It
mixes poorly. Conditional on $\bb$, a large $\lambda_k$ forces the difference
$b_{k+1} - b_k$ to be small, and conditional on a small difference the
posterior of $\tau_k$ favours an even larger precision, so the chain creeps
along a funnel. The remedy is to integrate the coefficients out: given
$\btau$ and $\sigma^2$ the model is linear and Gaussian, and the integral is
available in closed form.

Let $\btheta = (\tilde{\bb}, \bgamma)$ collect the $K - m$ penalised and $q$
unpenalised coefficients, let $\bX$ be the corresponding $n \times (K - m +
q)$ design, and define the sufficient statistics $\bS = \bX^\top \bX$,
$\mathbf{s} = \bX^\top \by$ and $\by^\top \by$, the block-diagonal prior
precision $\bQ_\theta = \mbox{blockdiag}(\tilde{\bQ}, \bzero)$, $\bA =
\bQ_\theta + \bS / \sigma^2$ and $\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{s} / \sigma^2$. Then
\begin{equation}
\log p(\by \mid \btau, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2} \log(2\pi\sigma^2)
+ \frac{q}{2} \log 2\pi + \frac{1}{2} \log |\tilde{\bQ}|_{+}
- \frac{1}{2} \log |\bA| + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{m}^\top \bA^{-1} \mathbf{m}
- \frac{\by^\top \by}{2 \sigma^2},
\label{eq:collapsed}
\end{equation}
where $|\cdot|_{+}$ denotes the generalised determinant, the product of the
non-zero eigenvalues, which is needed when $\tilde{\bQ}$ is singular. The
prior normalising constant is the only awkward term, and it has a simple
form. Without end constraints $\bQ = \bD^\top \mbox{diag}(\blambda) \bD$ is
the weighted Laplacian of the chain graph on $K$ nodes, and the matrix-tree
theorem \citep{kirchhoff1847, rue2005} gives $|\bQ|_{+} = K \prod_{k=1}^{K-1}
\lambda_k$, so that $\log |\bQ|_{+} = \log K + \sum_k \tau_k$. With a single
constraint of unit normal $\mathbf{v}$, $|\tilde{\bQ}| = |\bQ|_{+}
(\mathbf{v}^\top \bone / \sqrt{K})^2$, because $\bZ^\top \bQ \bZ$ is a
congruence of $\bQ$ restricted to a hyperplane whose determinant differs
from $|\bQ|_{+}$ only by the squared cosine of the angle between the two
hyperplane normals. In both cases $\log |\tilde{\bQ}|$ equals $\sum_k
\tau_k$ plus a constant, so every $\tau_k$ enters the normalising constant
linearly; with two constraints the determinant is evaluated numerically by a
Cholesky factorisation.

Evaluating Equation \eqref{eq:collapsed} therefore costs a single Cholesky
factorisation of the $(K - m + q)$-dimensional matrix $\bA$, of order $K^3$,
and does not depend on $n$ at all, since the sufficient statistics are
computed once. The unpenalised coefficients are integrated out together with
$\tilde{\bb}$, which is why $\bQ_\theta$ carries a zero block and
Equation \eqref{eq:collapsed} carries the term $(q/2) \log 2\pi$. The
derivative of Equation \eqref{eq:collapsed} with respect to $\tau_k$ is
$\frac{1}{2}\{1 - \lambda_k\, \mathrm{E}[(b_{k+1} - b_k)^2 \mid \by, \btau,
\sigma^2]\}$, the expected version of the quantity that drives the
conditional update, which makes clear what collapsing achieves: the
expectation is taken over the coefficients instead of fixing them.

Serially correlated errors are accommodated at the same cost. If the errors
follow a stationary AR(1) process with coefficient $\phi$, prewhitening
replaces $\by$ and $\bX$ by $\mathbf{L}_\phi \by$ and $\mathbf{L}_\phi \bX$,
where $\mathbf{L}_\phi$ scales the first row by $\sqrt{1 - \phi^2}$ and
subtracts $\phi$ times the previous row from every other row. The whitened
sufficient statistics are quadratic in $\phi$: $\bS(\phi) = \bS_a + \phi
\bS_b + \phi^2 \bS_c$, with $\bS_a = \bX^\top \bX$, $\bS_b = -(\bX_{1:}^\top
\bX_{:-1} + \bX_{:-1}^\top \bX_{1:})$ and $\bS_c = \bX_{1:-1}^\top
\bX_{1:-1}$ in terms of the design with its first or last row removed, and
likewise for $\mathbf{s}(\phi)$ and $\by^\top \by$, so three sets of
statistics computed once serve every value of $\phi$, and Equation
\eqref{eq:collapsed} gains the term $\frac{1}{2} \log (1 - \phi^2)$.

Finally, when the true curve is exactly flat over a range of lags, Equation
\eqref{eq:collapsed} is flat in the corresponding log precisions beyond the
point where the penalty dominates the data, and only the prior holds them
down. Log precisions above 30 are treated as infinite smoothing in every term
of the likelihood, which keeps $\bA$ well conditioned without changing the
posterior appreciably.

\subsection{Sampling the hyperparameters}
\label{sec:nuts}

What remains is the marginal posterior of $\btau$, $\sigma^2$, $\zeta$ and,
with AR(1) errors, $\phi$: a smooth density in $K + 1$ or $K + 2$ dimensions.
It is sampled with the No-U-Turn Sampler \citep{hoffman2014}, with gradients
obtained by automatic differentiation. Three details matter in practice.
First, the random walk prior is parameterised non-centrally: with $w_1 = 0$,
$w_{k+1} = w_k + \epsilon_k$ and $\epsilon_k \sim \N(0, 1)$, the log
precisions are $\btau = \bar{\tau} \bone + \zeta (\mathbf{w} - \bar{w}
\bone)$, so that $\zeta$ and the increments are a priori independent and the
posterior has no funnel in $\zeta$. The scale and the level are sampled on
the log scale and $\phi$ through its inverse hyperbolic tangent. Second, the
posterior of $\btau$ is a long ridge, since neighbouring log precisions are
tied together by the random walk, and a dense mass matrix adapted during
warm-up reduces the length of the trajectories several-fold. Third, the
chains start at the posterior mode, found by quasi-Newton optimisation in the
same parameterisation; a Gaussian approximation about that mode, with
covariance from the Hessian, also serves as a fast approximate fit.

The coefficients are recovered after sampling. For each retained draw of
$(\btau, \sigma^2, \phi)$ the coefficients are drawn from their conditional
distribution $\N(\bA^{-1} \mathbf{m}, \bA^{-1})$ using the Cholesky factor
of $\bA$, and the same factor gives the effective degrees of freedom of the
lag curve, $\mbox{ED} = \tr[(\bS + \sigma^2 \bQ_\theta)^{-1} \bS] - q$, the
trace of the hat matrix with the unpenalised terms, which contribute exactly
one degree of freedom each, removed. Draws of the lag curve $\bbeta =
\mathbf{B} \bZ \tilde{\bb}$ then yield pointwise credible bands, the
cumulative effect $\sum_j \beta_j$, the lag of the largest effect and the
lag beyond which the curve is negligible, all with their posterior
uncertainty.

The implementation was checked in three ways. Equation \eqref{eq:collapsed}
agrees to numerical precision with the density of $\by$ under its implied
marginal Gaussian distribution, with and without AR(1) errors. Simulation-based
calibration \citep{talts2018} over data sets simulated from the prior gives
uniform ranks for the error variance, the mean log precision and the lag
coefficients. And a Gibbs sampler with slice updates of the log precisions,
validated by the joint-distribution test of \citet{geweke2004}, yields the
same posterior as the collapsed sampler on common data.

\subsection{Software}
\label{sec:software}

The method is implemented in the Python package \texttt{badlm}
(\url{https://github.com/alastairrushworth/badlm}), built on JAX
\citep{jax2018} and NumPyro \citep{phan2019}. A fit with $n = 450$, $p = 50$
and $K = 34$ takes about 20 seconds for one chain of 300 warm-up and 300
retained draws on four processor cores, and about 30 seconds for four chains
of 2000 iterations; the cost grows with $K$ rather than $n$. The package also
provides Poisson and negative binomial responses, for which the coefficients
cannot be integrated out and are sampled jointly with the log precisions;
these count models sample markedly less efficiently, for the reason given at
the start of Section \ref{sec:collapse}, and a collapsed scheme for them is
the subject of further work.

\section{Simulation study}
\label{sec:sim}

The adaptive model detailed in Section \ref{sec:model} was compared with four
currently available distributed lag models, labelled $\M{1}$, $\M{2}$,
$\M{4}$ and $\M{5}$, with the adaptive model as $\M{3}$. $\M{1}$ is the
non-adaptive version of the model of Section \ref{sec:model}, achieved by
fixing $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \ldots = \lambda_{K-1} = \lambda$. $\M{2}$ is
a Bayesian implementation of the DLM of \citet{muggeo2008}, in which the
basis coefficients are shrunk by a ridge penalty that increases linearly with
lag, $\bQ = \lambda\, \mbox{diag}(1, 2, \ldots, K)$. $\M{4}$ is the P-spline
model of \citet{obermeier2015}, with the first-difference penalty of $\M{1}$
plus a ridge penalty, $\bQ = \lambda \bP + \rho \mathbf{I}$. $\M{5}$ is the
DLM with logarithmically spaced knots of \citet{gasparrini2010}, fitted by
least squares with the number of interior knots, between zero and twelve,
chosen by AIC. Models $\M{1}$ to $\M{4}$ share the same basis of $K =
\lceil 2(p + 1)/3 \rceil$ uniformly spaced cubic B-splines with the curve
constrained to zero at the maximum lag, so that they differ only in the
penalty; their scale parameters $\lambda$ and $\rho$ carry the penalised
complexity prior of Section \ref{sec:model} that the adaptive model places on
the level of its log precisions. Every Bayesian model was fitted with the
collapsed sampler of Section \ref{sec:comp}, with one chain of 300 retained
draws after 300 warm-up iterations started at the posterior mode, and the
posterior mean of the lag curve was taken as the estimate. Effective degrees
of freedom are those of the lag curve, and for $\M{5}$ the number of basis
functions selected. The study is reproduced by a script in the software
repository.

\subsection{Comparison under different lag structures}
\label{sec:sim1}

Time series response and covariate data were simulated under each of five
lag relationships with maximum lag $p = 50$, shown in Figure
\ref{fig:curves}: a delayed peak scenario (\scen{Delayed peak}), an
exponential decay curve (\scen{Decay curve}), a horizontal line at zero
equivalent to a null effect (\scen{Flat response}), a curve exhibiting a shape
similar to a mortality displacement effect (\scen{Displacement}) and a
sharply peaked function that declines rapidly to zero (\scen{Sharp peak}).
A covariate series was generated as $x_t \sim \N(0.5 x_{t-1}, 0.1^2)$ and
standardised, and conditional on each lag function a response series was
generated as $y_t = \sum_{j=0}^{50} \beta_j x_{t-j} + \epsilon_t$ for $t =
51, \ldots, 500$, with autocorrelated errors $\epsilon_t \sim \N(0.2
\epsilon_{t-1}, 0.1^2)$. The lag function explains about 30 per cent of the
variance of $y_t$ under the \scen{Delayed peak} curve. The autocorrelation
in both $x_t$ and $\epsilon_t$ reflects the temporal dependence in covariates
and errors typically observed in real data.

\label{fig:curves}
\end{figure}

Models $\M{1}$ to $\M{5}$ were fitted to each of 50 realisations of the
processes $\{x_t, y_t\}$ described above. The models were assessed in terms
of their ability to recover the true lag curve, as measured by root mean
squared error (RMSE) and squared bias (Bias$^2$) of the estimates, defined
for the $j$th simulation as $\left(\frac{1}{51} \sum_{i=0}^{50}
(\hat{\beta}_i^{(j)} - \beta_i)^2\right)^{1/2}$ and $\left(\frac{1}{51}
\sum_{i=0}^{50} (\hat{\beta}_i^{(j)} - \beta_i)\right)^2$, and, following
\citet{hodges2001}, by the effective degrees of freedom of the fitted curve.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\input{tables/simulation.tex}
\caption{Estimated lag function RMSE, squared bias and effective degrees of
freedom for five types of lag curve shape, averaged across 50 simulated data
sets. RMSE has been scaled by $10^3$ and Bias$^2$ by $10^6$.}
\label{tab:sim}
\end{table}

Table \ref{tab:sim} summarises the results. The adaptive model $\M{3}$ has
the lowest RMSE under each of the four lag curves with a non-null effect,
and the margin is widest where the curve changes character along the lag
axis. Relative to the best of the other four models its RMSE is 23 per cent
lower under the \scen{Delayed peak} curve, 24 per cent lower under
\scen{Sharp peak}, 17 per cent lower under \scen{Displacement} and 11 per
cent lower under \scen{Decay curve}, and in each of these scenarios it
attains the lower error in at least 96 per cent of the 50 replicates when
compared replicate by replicate with $\M{1}$, $\M{2}$ or $\M{4}$. The
single-penalty P-spline $\M{1}$ is the strongest of the other Bayesian
models under the three smooth curves, but under \scen{Sharp peak}, where one
smoothing parameter must compromise between the steep rise at short lags
and the long flat tail, it and $\M{4}$ are the worst models of all, with
RMSE more than double that of $\M{3}$. The ridge component of $\M{4}$ adds a little error to $\M{1}$ in
every scenario, and the lag-increasing ridge of $\M{2}$ does so under the
three smooth curves, although it helps under \scen{Sharp peak}, whose effect
is confined to the short lags that its penalty leaves relatively free. The
log-knot model $\M{5}$ is competitive under \scen{Decay curve} and
\scen{Sharp peak}, whose features lie at the short lags where its knots are
dense, but it has the largest error of all under \scen{Delayed peak}, whose
feature lies at intermediate lags, and under \scen{Flat response}, where AIC
selects spurious structure. Under that null curve the four Bayesian models
are close, and $\M{1}$ and $\M{3}$ are indistinguishable, each using about
2.5 effective degrees of freedom: the adaptive prior does not invent
structure when there is none to find. Under the four non-null curves the
squared bias is a small part of the mean squared error for every model, so
the differences in RMSE reflect the variance of the estimates rather than
systematic error in their level; under \scen{Flat response}, where the
fitted curves are almost straight lines, the error of a Bayesian model is
mostly a small shift in the level of the curve, which the ridge penalties of
$\M{2}$ and $\M{4}$ shrink towards zero.

The effective degrees of freedom show the mechanism. $\M{3}$ uses fewer
effective parameters than $\M{1}$, $\M{2}$ and $\M{4}$ under every curve,
and under the four non-null curves it uses between a fifth and three fifths
fewer than $\M{1}$ (for example 5.7 against 14.0 under \scen{Sharp peak} and
8.8 against 13.3 under \scen{Delayed peak}). Lower error with fewer
effective parameters is the signature of adaptive smoothing: the lag-varying
precision is high, and the fitted curve close to linear, over the flat part
of each curve, and is relaxed only where the curve actually moves. The
price is computational. With the collapsed sampler of Section
\ref{sec:comp} a fit of $\M{3}$ took about 25 seconds on a single CPU core
against 10 to 13 seconds for $\M{1}$, $\M{2}$ and $\M{4}$, whose
hyperparameter spaces have one or two dimensions rather than $K - 1$, and
well under a second for the least squares fit of $\M{5}$.

\subsection{DLM misspecification with maximum lag $p$}
\label{sec:sim2}

Next, simulation was used to explore the robustness of $\M{1}$ to $\M{5}$ to
different choices of maximum lag $p$. The series $x_t$ and $y_t$ were
generated exactly as in Section \ref{sec:sim1}, using only the
\scen{Displacement} lag function with 50 lags, and the models were fitted
assuming maximum lags of $p = 50, 75, 100$ and $125$, with $K = \lceil 2(p +
1)/3 \rceil$ basis functions so that the flexibility available to each model
grows in proportion to $p$. For each of 20 realisations the RMSE of the lag
curve estimate over all $p + 1$ lags, its squared bias and the effective
degrees of freedom were recorded; Figure \ref{fig:ed} summarises the
results.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/fig2_ed_rmse.pdf}
\caption{Effective degrees of freedom and RMSE for each of $\M{1}$ to
$\M{5}$. Increasing diameter of the plotting symbols indicates larger
numbers of maximum lags assumed in advance of model fitting.}
\label{fig:ed}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:ed} shows that the adaptive model is almost indifferent to
the choice of maximum lag. Its effective degrees of freedom rise only from
9.1 at $p = 50$ to 10.9 at $p = 125$, although the number of basis
functions, and so the flexibility on offer, grows from 34 to 84: the random
walk prior on the log precisions settles on a high precision across the
added lags, where the data show nothing, and the fitted curve there is a
straight line at zero. Its RMSE, which averages the squared error over all
$p + 1$ lags, consequently falls from 1.40 to 1.11, because the added lags
are estimated almost without error and dilute the error in the first 50,
and $\M{3}$ has the lowest error of the five models at every $p$, in every
one of the 20 replicates when compared with $\M{1}$, $\M{2}$ or $\M{4}$. The
single-penalty P-spline $\M{1}$ and the P-spline with ridge $\M{4}$ behave
quite differently: their effective degrees of freedom grow almost in
proportion to $p$, from 12.9 to 23.5 and from 15.1 to 31.1, because the one
smoothing parameter must stay small enough to follow the
\scen{Displacement} shape over the first 50 lags and then lets the curve
wander over the lags that follow, and their RMSE rises with $p$ even though
the added lags should be easy to estimate. The lag-increasing ridge of
$\M{2}$ does shrink the distant lags, so its RMSE falls with $p$, but its
effective degrees of freedom still climb to 27 and its error at $p = 125$
remains about 50 per cent above that of $\M{3}$. The log-knot model $\M{5}$
keeps its degrees of freedom roughly constant by construction, because its
knots are concentrated at short lags, but it is the least accurate model of
all at $p = 50$ and $75$. The cost of the adaptive model's $K - 1$
hyperparameters is again computational: its fitting time grows from 28
seconds at $p = 50$ to about two minutes at $p = 125$, against 11 to 19
seconds for the other Bayesian models at every $p$.

\section{Illustrations with real data}
\label{sec:real}

Four applications illustrate how the adaptive lag curve behaves on noisy real
data. All use the accompanying software with its default settings: the random
walk prior on the log precisions, an exact zero constraint at the maximum lag,
$K = \lceil 2(p+1)/3 \rceil$ basis functions, and the collapsed sampler of
Section \ref{sec:comp} with two chains of 1000 retained draws. Effective degrees of freedom are
reported for the lag curve alone, excluding unpenalised terms. The scripts
that reproduce them are in the software repository.

\subsection{Daily stock market data}

Prices are not stationary, so they are transformed to percentage log returns
$r_t = 100 \log(P_t / P_{t-1})$. Daily closing prices of the S\&P 500 index and
of Apple, and closing values of the VIX implied volatility index, were obtained
for 2007 to 2016, 2,306 trading days spanning the 2008 crisis. Figure
\ref{fig:stocks} shows four lag curves. Panels (a) and (b) regress the next
day's return on the preceding 20 days' returns, the linear information that a
trading rule could exploit. For the index the curve is negative at lag one,
$-0.10$ (95\% interval $-0.15$ to $-0.06$) per percentage point, and returns to zero within a week: a short-lived
reversal with cumulative effect $-0.23$ ($-0.42$ to $-0.03$) and 4.7 effective degrees of freedom.
For Apple the curve is indistinguishable from zero at every lag, with 1.7
effective degrees of freedom; the adaptive smoother reports an absence of
structure rather than inventing it. Panel (c) regresses the daily change in
log VIX on the same day's and the preceding 60 days' index returns. The
contemporaneous coefficient is $-4.1$ ($-4.2$ to $-3.9$), so implied volatility rises about
4 per cent for each percentage point the index falls; it is followed
by a small positive rebound over the following week as the shock partly
reverses, and by nothing beyond about ten days. Panel (d) regresses the log
absolute return, a measure of realised volatility, on its own past over 100
days. The curve is strongest at lags two to three, decays over about a month
and remains small but mostly positive thereafter, the long memory of
volatility; the cumulative elasticity is 0.84 (0.75 to 0.94) and the smoother
describes 100 lags with 7.9 effective degrees of freedom. In each panel the band widens where the data are least informative
and the curve is pinned to zero at the maximum lag.

\subsection{Intraday order flow and prices}

At the other extreme of time scale, the sample data distributed with the
QuantConnect Lean repository contain every trade and quote for the exchange
traded fund SPY and for the stock BAC on the five trading days from 7 to 11
October 2013. They were reduced to one-second series: the midquote from the
best bid and offer of the four most active venues, its return in basis
points, the order-flow imbalance (the number of buyer-initiated minus
seller-initiated trades, signed against the prevailing midquote), volume and
the quoted spread. Lags were formed within each day, so that the first second
of a day is never regressed on the previous afternoon, and B-splines of time
of day entered as unpenalised terms to absorb the intraday pattern in volume
and volatility. Each series has 117,000 seconds, and all fits took the
default settings. Figure \ref{fig:micro} shows the results.

Panel (a) regresses the midquote return on the order-flow imbalance of the
same second and the 120 before it, and panel (b) shows the cumulative sum of
these coefficients, the response of the price to one unit of order flow as a
function of time since the trade, with its credible band. One more buy than
sell trade moves the midquote by 0.0061 basis points in the same second; the
response continues to build for about seven seconds, to 0.0093, and about 15
per cent of it then decays away over the following minute, leaving a
permanent impact of 0.0079 (0.0074 to 0.0084). This is the shape that
microstructure models describe with a transient propagator
\citep{hasbrouck1991, bouchaud2004}, estimated here without assuming its
functional form. Panels (c) and (f) regress the imbalance on its own past
over 300 seconds for SPY and BAC. The coefficient at one second is 0.16 for
SPY and 0.11 for BAC, but the tails carry most of the memory: the
coefficients over five minutes sum to 0.38 and 0.57 respectively, and the
curve for BAC, a large-tick stock whose queue dynamics encourage order
splitting, remains positive to about 100 seconds \citep{lillo2004}. Panels
(d) and (e) show the two directions of the relation between volume and
volatility: a thousand shares traded a second earlier raise the next second's
absolute return by 0.001 basis points, with a decay over about a minute and a
secondary bump at six to seven seconds that coincides with the time taken to
absorb order flow in panel (b); and one basis point of absolute return attracts
about 1,400 additional shares in the following second and some 22,000 over
the following two minutes. The six curves use between 8 and 12 effective
degrees of freedom for 120 to 300 lags.

\subsection{Air pollution and mortality in Chicago}

The setting that motivated much of the distributed lag literature is the
delayed effect of air pollution on daily mortality, and in particular the
question of mortality displacement raised in Section \ref{sec:intro}. The
\texttt{chicagoNMMAPS} data distributed with the R package \texttt{dlnm}
\citep{gasparrini2011} contain daily counts of all-cause, cardiovascular and
respiratory deaths in Chicago from 1987 to 2000, 5,114 days with means of 115,
51 and 9 deaths per day, together with PM10 (mean 34 micrograms per cubic
metre, 5 per cent of days interpolated), temperature and dew point. Log deaths
were regressed on 40 lags of PM10 with the usual adjustments of this
literature as unpenalised terms: a B-spline of time with seven degrees of
freedom per year for trend and season, weekday indicators, and B-splines of
temperature and dew point, with AR(1) errors. Coefficients are expressed as the
percentage change in deaths per 10 micrograms per cubic metre.

Figure \ref{fig:chicago} shows the three lag curves and their cumulative
sums. For all-cause deaths the curve is positive on the day of exposure, 0.15
per cent (95\% interval $-0.02$ to $0.36$), falls through zero within three
days and is negative from about lag four to lag ten, where the band excludes
zero, before returning to zero. The cumulative effect over 40 days is
$-0.20$ per cent ($-1.30$ to $0.85$): deaths are brought forward by a few days
rather than added, the displacement pattern of \citet{schwartz2000} and
\citet{zanobetti2000}, and the lag curve exhibits it without any lag structure
having been specified in advance. The curve uses about five effective degrees
of freedom. Cardiovascular deaths show the same shape with smaller amplitude,
and respiratory deaths, at nine per day, are too few to show anything beyond
a wide band around zero.

\subsection{COVID-19 cases and deaths}

Deaths from COVID-19 follow infections after a delay of a few weeks, so the
lag curve relating daily deaths to earlier cases estimates both the size of
the relationship and the shape of the delay. Daily counts for the United
States from June 2020 to March 2021, 304 days with a mean of 1,471 deaths per
day, were taken from the compilation by The New York Times; the period
precedes any effect of vaccination on the case fatality ratio. The exposure is
the log of the seven-day trailing mean of new cases and the response is log
deaths, so the lag coefficients are elasticities. Weekday indicators absorb
the day-of-week pattern in death reporting and the errors follow an AR(1)
process to allow for the remaining serial correlation. Figure \ref{fig:covid}
shows the data and the fitted curve with $p = 35$. The cumulative elasticity
is 0.74 (0.66 to 0.81): a 10 per cent rise in cases is followed by roughly a 7 per
cent rise in deaths, the shortfall from one reflecting the fall in the case
fatality ratio over the period. The curve rises to a peak at about three weeks
and returns to zero by five, using under three effective degrees of freedom,
but the individual lag coefficients are poorly identified, because the smoothed case series is highly collinear across lags:
the data determine the total and the timing, and the adaptive prior supplies
the smoothness. Deaths reported on Tuesday to Friday are about 2.5 times
those reported on a Sunday. A negative binomial model for the raw counts,
shown dashed in Figure \ref{fig:covid}, agrees on the total elasticity, 0.73
(0.68 to 0.77), and on the peak, but its sampler did not converge
satisfactorily, with many divergent transitions and $\hat{R}$ of 1.4: the
dependence between the curve and its smoothing parameters that the Gaussian
model integrates out remains present in a count model, and a collapsed or
approximately collapsed scheme for count responses is a topic for further
work.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This paper makes several important contributions. It was shown by simulation
in Section \ref{sec:sim} that estimation of lag structure can strongly depend
on the type of smoothing model that is assumed. Several existing DLM models
were shown to be non-robust to the choice of maximum lag $p$, even when the
underlying lag function is identical, which suggests that the interpretation
of lag estimates should be made with caution. A new model has been proposed
that combines automatic adaptive smoothing with a pragmatically large choice
of $p$ to ensure simple and flexible smoothing of the lag curve that avoids
sensitivity to the choice of $p$, together with an inference scheme in which
the lag coefficients are integrated out analytically, so that the remaining
posterior is low dimensional, free of the funnel that hampers a Gibbs sampler,
and sampled at a cost that does not grow with the length of the series. The real data illustrations in Section
\ref{sec:real} show the adaptive curve reporting no structure where the data
contain none, a sharp and short-lived response where there is one, a slowly
decaying response over a hundred lags, the build-up and partial decay of price
impact over seconds, and the displacement of deaths by a few days after a
polluted day, in each case with far fewer effective parameters than the number
of lags. The new approach provides users
of DLMs with a new way to explore their data with confidence that the
estimates are not contaminated by artefacts that result from particular model
choices.
